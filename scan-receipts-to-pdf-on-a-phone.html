Scan receipts to one PDF on a phone

You do not need a scanning app to get a pile of paper receipts into a single PDF from your phone. This page is that step — it runs in the browser tab on the phone, with no app, no account and nothing uploaded.

The scanner is already in your hand

A phone camera photographs a till receipt well enough to read: the merchant, the date, the tax line and the total all come out legible on a plain surface in ordinary light. What a phone cannot do on its own is put a week of those photos into one file that opens the same way every time.

So the workflow is two halves. Photograph the receipts with the phone, and assemble those photos into one PDF here. Nothing is installed for the second half, because it is a web page: the browser that opened this page already has everything it needs to make the file.

What you do, in order

1. Photograph each receipt
One photo per receipt, the receipt filling the frame, on a plain surface. Do it when you get the receipt — a thermal till receipt left in a drawer or a car can fade to unreadable within a year. Where you can, let the filename carry the date (IMG_20260305_1420.jpg); the tool reads it back out.
2. Open the tool on the phone
Open this site in the phone's browser and tap choose files. That opens the browser's own photo picker — the same one any website uses — and the pictures come straight from the camera roll. Pick the whole batch at once.
3. Check the list it makes
Each photo becomes a row with a note, a date and an amount. Where the date came out of the filename the row says so; where it could not, you type it in. Turn a sideways photo with the ↻ button, move a row with the arrows, remove a mistake with the ×.
4. Build the PDF
Give the file a title if you want one (“Receipts, March”), pick Letter or A4, and press Build the PDF. It appears in the phone's downloads as one dated, indexed PDF, ready to attach to a claim or drop in a folder.

What this does not do

It is worth being plain about the word “scan”, because a scanning app means something this tool is not. This tool does not read the receipt: it does not OCR the merchant or the total, it does not detect the edges of the paper and crop to them, and it does not guess an amount. It does not need to — the photo is the receipt, and one clear photo per page is what makes a receipt file worth keeping.

What it does is assemble the photos you give it: a cover index that lists every receipt in date order with its amount and a total for the batch, then one full page per receipt with its date and amount in the footer. The one thing it reads by itself is a date in the filename. Every other number is one you type on the row.

Why the photos never leave the phone

Receipts carry your card number, your address and what you buy, which is exactly the sort of picture you would rather not hand to a stranger's server. There is no server here to hand it to: no backend, no API key, no analytics. The page ships a content-security policy whose connect-src 'none' directive blocks every network request, so the photos could not be uploaded even if a bug tried to. The PDF is assembled by the JavaScript already running in the tab, and the pictures are read from the phone by the browser itself. The full source is on GitHub.

Questions

Do I need to install a scanning app?
No. Your phone's camera is the scanner; this is a web page, not an app, so there is nothing to install and nothing to sign up for.
Does this app read the receipt for me?
No. It does not scan or OCR the receipt and does not guess an amount. It gathers the photos you give it into one PDF, reads a date out of the filename when there is one, and takes the amount you type on each row.
Do my photos get uploaded from my phone?
No. There is no upload and no server to upload to. The page ships a content-security policy that blocks every network request, so the photos are read and the PDF is assembled in your own browser tab.
Does it work on an iPhone or an Android phone?
It is an ordinary web page, so it opens in whatever browser is already on the phone. The photo picker is the browser's own, the same one any site uses, so the pictures come straight from the camera roll.
