How to organize receipts for taxes

Organizing receipts is a filing job before it is anything else: get each one into a dated, ordered pile you can hand over. This is the system that costs nothing and does not depend on any app keeping your data — one dated photo per receipt, one folder per tax year, one PDF that lists them in order with a total. It happens in the browser tab you already have open.

Why the shoebox fails

Two things go wrong with receipts before the tax question even comes up. First, the paper fades: a thermal till receipt is unreadable within a year or two if it is left in a drawer or a car. Second, the pile has no order — a bag of paper, or a camera roll of photos named IMG_4821.jpg, tells you nothing about which receipt is from when.

A system only has to fix those two things. Photograph every receipt while the ink is still there, put a date on it, and keep the year's receipts together in one file. That is the whole job.

A system in four steps

1. Capture at the moment
Photograph a receipt as you get it, not at tax time. A phone photo of a fresh receipt is legible; the same receipt in a drawer for a year may not be. One photo per receipt, straight on, on a plain surface.
2. Put the date in the filename
The date is the one thing a photo of a receipt loses. Naming the file with its date is what lets the pile sort itself later — the tool reads the date straight out of the filename (examples below). Where it cannot, you type the date into the row.
3. Keep one file per tax year
Drop the year's photos in, build a single PDF, and keep it next to that year's return. One file, one year, in date order — not four hundred loose images in a folder.
4. Keep the index with the file
The PDF opens with a cover page that lists every receipt in order with its date and amount, then a total for the batch. That is the page you or your accountant scan first to see the year at a glance before going into the individual receipts.

Where this tool fits

Where the dates come from

Anything genuinely ambiguous — receipt_03-04-2026.jpg could be 4 March or 3 April — is left blank rather than guessed, and you type it in the row. A batch is sorted by the date each receipt carries as it loads, so dropping a pile in any order still gives you a PDF that reads oldest to newest.

What this does not do

This is a filing method, not tax advice. It puts your receipts in one ordered file with a total; it does not decide what you can claim, does not compute a deduction, and does not know the rules for where you live. Whether an expense is claimable, and how long you should keep the file afterwards, is a question for your accountant or your tax authority — not for this page.

The tool itself makes no tax claim either. It builds the PDF. That is all.

Questions

Do I need an app that connects to my bank?
No. Nothing here connects anywhere — not to a bank, not to a server. You photograph the receipts you have and the file is built in your own browser.
Can I do a whole year in one PDF?
Yes. On the unlocked tier there is no limit. On the free tier the PDF is built from the first 5 receipts in the list, and the result tells you how many were left out.
Can I use receipts I already have as PDFs?
This tool takes photos and image files (JPG, PNG, WebP, GIF), not PDFs. If a receipt is already a PDF, you would turn its page into an image first, then add it like any other.
Where does the file end up?
In your browser's downloads, on your own machine. Nothing about the batch is uploaded or stored anywhere — the file is assembled by the JavaScript in the tab.
What does it cost?
Free for a PDF of up to 5 receipts, index and totals included, no account and no email. A one-time $9 unlock removes the 5-receipt limit and the small footer mark on each page.
